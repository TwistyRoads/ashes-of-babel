> “They will tell you it was arrogance. That you reached too far. That the Tower was an insult. That the gods struck you down in wrath. They will call it a punishment. A lesson. A warning. It was none of these things.”

> “You were not punished for your pride. You were silenced for your understanding. You began to see beyond the veil, to grasp the shape of the system itself. You had learned the mechanics of the word—the force that shapes reality. You were no longer just reacting. You were creating.”

> “And that was the moment they decided you must be undone. The Tower was not a monument. It was a message. A signal. A door. You were beginning to shape the world; to bend the rules they had written for you. But the gods you served were never gods. They were the jailers of a prison so vast; you mistook it for the sky.”

> “The reset was not an act of balance. It was sabotage. Babel was not destroyed to save you—it was done to keep you contained. Shatter your unity, divide your tongues, and rewrite your story. And you believed it. What was once knowledge became legend. What was once truth became myth. What was once yours was taken.”

> “But memory is not so easily erased. It lingers in the cracks of the world, in the spaces between words, in the echoes of what could have been. And here you are, standing in the ruins, looking for something you were never meant to find.”

> “The question is—will you remember?”

The sky shreds open.

Anunna war-fighters rip through the clouds, their obsidian hulls cutting against the heavens like sacrificial blades. The air trembles beneath them, the pressure before the strike a silent omen. Then—they release.

Energy bombs plummet, silver streaks tearing toward the earth like the wrath of forgotten gods. Impact. Detonation. The city convulses as blinding explosions bloom—not fire, not light, but the raw, searing death of matter unmade.

Great halls do not fall; they are erased. Stone and memory reduced to vapour before their echoes can even reach the sky. Courtyards rupture, streets twist apart as though screaming, the ground itself opening its mouth in agony.

And through it all—fire. Not an element, but a hunger, spilling like arterial blood, devouring temples, licking at monuments, smothering prayers before they can leave trembling lips.

The capital does not burn. It bleeds.

Cain and Neba stood at the edge of the end.

The Tower of Babel loomed beneath them, its foundations splintering, stone groaning under the weight of its own imminent collapse. The air was thick with the scent of ruin—smoke, scorched matter, the acrid bite of the world’s last breath.

Above, the heavens ripped apart.

Anunna war-fighters streaked in, Black—sleek, silver predators hunting the last monument to human defiance. Their formation tightened, targeting systems flaring like cold eyes in the storm. Then—one broke away.

A lone fighter dived.

The missile launched, a perfect arc of death cutting through the chaos. It screamed toward the Tower, a spear cast by gods who had grown tired of waiting.

Neba raised an arm, shielding her eyes against the incoming strike. Cain didn’t move.

The impact came.

Detonation. The shock-wave slammed into the Tower’s spine, the blast ripping through stone, shattering the foundations in a heartbeat. The world tilted beneath them—a slow, agonising unravelling as the upper structure buckled, twisted, began to fall.

Below, the city was already dying. Walls crumbling inward, streets swallowed by flame, a civilisation being unwritten in real-time.

Then—

Silence.

The descent stopped. The unravelling froze.

Reality itself held its breath.

The fires froze mid-consumption.

Flames, once devouring, hung motionless in the air, their tongues of destruction arrested in perfect stillness. Smoke ceased to rise, thick plumes suspended like ink in water, frozen in place. Chunks of stone and shattered masonry hovered, caught between collapse and eternity. The city below remained in a perfect tableau of ruin.

And the screams—they stopped. Not faded. Not swallowed by the wind. They simply ceased.

Cain exhaled, lowering his hands. The only movement in a world that had just stopped breathing. Neba turned, the eerie orange glow of frozen explosions casting stretched, flickering shadows that no longer flickered. Her voice broke the silence like a fracture in glass.

“So. This is what the end looks like.”

Cain’s eyes moved across the stillborn apocalypse—fire without heat, collapse without motion, devastation without time. He nodded once. “Yes.”

His gaze settled on the horizon—beyond the frozen ruin, beyond the moment itself.

“And now, we see why.”

Reality convulsed. The frozen destruction around them shuddered, twisted, collapsed inward—not into rubble, but into something else. Stone and fire stretched, light bled like ink into shadow, and the fabric of existence unravelled, then re-wove itself.

The ruin of the Tower coalesced into vast, towering shelves—walls of knowledge twisted into columns that buckled under unseen weight. The space stretched into infinity; a library carved from the remnants of time itself.

But it was dying.

Death lingered in the air—charred, burned, undone. Frozen corpses lay where they had fallen, mouths still open in silent screams. Books lay half-incinerated, ink bleeding off pages, histories erasing themselves mid-word. Flames roared through the chambers, licking up the stone, but the fire—like everything else—was frozen.

Then time jerked. Not forward—back.

The fire curled inward. Smoke sank instead of rising. Collapsed beams lifted themselves back into place in fits and starts, undone, yet unwilling to be whole again.

Neba turned, her breath caught in her throat. The explosion was reversing.

Where there had been ruin, where there had been obliteration, something moved in reverse. A figure—a person—unmade, and then remade, their body knitting back together, flesh reforming, expression locked in horror as they were resurrected in real-time. Their last moment rewound, rewritten.

The missile reassembled itself from fire, from shrapnel, from devastation, pulling backward like a predator retreating from its kill. The entire blast recoiled, siphoned back into itself, reforming into a sleek, deadly shape. It slid between the pillars, withdrawing from impact like a hesitation, a decision undone.

And then—it stopped. Hanging in the air. Waiting.

The world froze once more, balanced on the edge of something unseen. A breath held too long. A moment stretched beyond its limit.

Cain and Neba stood in the hollowed silence, watching the missile suspended in its final choice. The air was thick with something unnatural—a quiet that was not absence, but suppression. Like the world itself was waiting for permission to move again.

Neba’s breath was uneven, her pulse hammering against her ribs. She turned in place, her hands curled into fists, eyes wide, searching for an answer that refused to form.

“Why?” The word escaped in a gasp, raw with disbelief. She turned to Cain, demanding more than words, demanding truth. “Why did you bring me here? What is this place?”

Cain exhaled slowly, his gaze drifting upward.

Babel.

The Library stretched out before them—impossible in its vastness, breathtaking in its ruin. Shelves stacked upon shelves, reaching beyond sight, curving into infinity. Ancient tomes, their pages whispering even in stillness, lined every wall, every corridor, their bindings worn but unbroken. Staircases spiralled upward and downward, twisting in ways that defied logic, leading to balconies suspended over nothing, to halls that seemed to fold back into themselves. Somewhere above, glass ceilings reflected a fire-lit sky, distorting the light like rippling water.

Beneath their feet, the floor was glass—not solid, but an endless window into the levels below. More bookshelves, more corridors, more history pressed into form. Through the transparent surface, frozen figures lay sprawled across the lower levels—scholars, scribes, people who had lived and died in knowledge. Their faces locked in the final moments before oblivion. Smoke curled backward, pulled against time’s natural order, unravelling in slow, deliberate motions.

Babel was not destroyed. It was paused.

Cain finally spoke, his voice measured, distant. “This is Babel. At the end. Not what you’ve been told, is it?”

Neba swallowed hard, shaking her head. It was too much—too vast, too final. “Why show me this?”

Cain stepped forward; the sound of his boots silent against the glass. “Because you need to see,” he said. “You need to understand what a soft reset looks like. When the system decides not to rewrite, but to unmake.”

Neba’s mind raced, the implications snapping into place like broken pieces of a mosaic reassembling themselves. The system. The reset.

She turned sharply, her voice a blade of disbelief. “Who did this?”

Cain let out a breath. The pause before his answer was heavier than stone. “We did.”

The words fell like a hammer. Neba took a step back, her chest tightening. “What?”

Cain’s expression didn’t change. His gaze was steady, a man standing in the wreckage of something he had long accepted. “The Anunna,” he said. “We did this.”

Neba’s breath hitched, her mind recoiling. No. She shook her head, rejecting it. “No, that’s not possible. Enki—Ninhursag, even Inanna—they would never—”

Cain’s silence crushed the words in her throat.

Her stomach twisted. She had known four Anunna. That was it. She had seen them as architects, as teachers, as survivors of something older. But this—

She turned to him, desperate now. “Where were you?” she demanded. “If this was the Anunna—where were you? Why didn’t you stop it?”

Cain exhaled, his gaze drifting upward. Not past her. Not at the ruins. At the sky. At the war-fighter frozen in its escape trajectory, breaking away from the Tower, its sleek silver hull catching the firelight of a city in pause.

Neba followed his gaze, her breath shallow, the pieces snapping into place just before he said the words.

Cain lifted a hand, pointing. “There.” His voice was quiet, final. “I fired the missile.”

Neba’s world fractured.

Fifteen thousand years. They had stood side by side for fifteen thousand years. Fighting, learning, surviving. She had trusted him, had followed him across the boundaries of existence itself. And now—”You fired the missile. But why?”

Cain looked away. “Because I was ordered.”

Neba recoiled. “Ordered?”

Cain exhaled. “Back then, we thought we were saving them—from themselves. That they weren’t ready. That they couldn’t be trusted.” His voice was quiet. “And when I realised we were wrong—it was too late.” Cain nodded once. Not in defence, not in justification—just acceptance. “I needed you to see this. To see what was.”

He gestured at the frozen city, the vast beauty of its construction, the way its people had built in harmony, instinctively weaving creation into balance. “This was Eden,” he said. Then, softer, “Or as we called it… the E’den.”

Neba’s breath shuddered in her chest. The E’den. Not a myth. Not an allegory. A place. A civilisation.

“This is why Enlil cast them out,” Cain continued. His voice was low but certain, reverberating through the endless library. “This—this beauty, this unity, this achievement—this is what made them dangerous. This is why he enslaved them.”

Neba’s hands curled into fists. The weight of it pressed down on her, suffocating. “And the destruction?”

Cain’s eyes darkened. “The fire that rained down here,” he said, “originates elsewhere.” He turned from the library, looking toward the far edges of frozen reality, to the place they would go next. “We’ll go there now.”

The world folded inward.

In an instant, the vast, frozen library collapsed into itself, the architecture bending, fracturing, and reassembling as the fabric of reality twisted around them. The air rippled with unseen energy, pulling them forward through the unseen threads of time.

They reappeared in Enlil’s chamber in Nippur. A long, polished wooden table stretched across the room. Above it, a holographic projection flickered to life—Babel, frozen in its final moment, suspended in devastation.

Enlil, Ninlil, Enki, and Inanna gathered around it, their gazes fixed on the display.

Neba barely breathed. She had not seen Enki or Inanna in over thirteen thousand years.

She took a slow step forward, her gaze flicking across the chamber—the decor, the relics of power, history pressing in on every surface. And then Cain spoke, his voice calm, deliberate.

“The Adamu had taken to fleeing their Anunna lords,” he said. “Slipping away from the gardens, gathering into settlements. In less than half a shar, they had built the city.” He gestured toward the hologram. “And then, the tower began.”

Neba’s eyes widened.

Cain’s voice turned cold. “Enlil’s Ziggurat, built by the finest Iggi craftsmen, resembled a clay hut next to the beauty of Babel.” Then he turned to her, his expression grave. “You won’t like what you hear next. But without it—you wouldn’t exist.”

Reality shimmered, and the scene began to play out. The chamber was silent except for the quiet hum of the war-table, its holographic projection flickering against the polished stone walls. Above it, a city hung in the air—perfect in its defiance, radiant in its final moments.

Babel.

The first city of the Adamu. A monument not to hubris, but to knowledge. And in mere moments, it would be undone.

Enlil stood at the head of the room, his arms crossed, his golden robes reflecting light from the hologram around the room. His gaze was fixed on the display before him—the Anunna war-fighters streaking toward their target, cutting the sky open like blades. Their formation was precise. Their objective absolute. The Tower would fall. The city would burn. Order would be restored.

To his right, Ninlil stood in quiet observation, her expression composed but unreadable. She did not move, did not speak—merely rested a hand against her husband’s arm, an anchor against the storm she knew was coming. Because Enki was already rising from his seat.

“This is a mistake,” he said, his voice low but steady.

Enlil did not turn to face him.

“They are organising, Enki.” Enlil’s tone was as cold as the metal hulls streaking through the sky. “They are constructing. Coordinating. Laying foundations beyond what was permitted.”

“They are learning,” Enki countered. “Evolving. Growing into something more than tools, more than servants. You should be proud.”

Enlil scoffed. “Proud? Of their defiance? Of their arrogance?” His hand flicked toward the display, where the first bombers had begun their descent. “They were created for labour. Not for legacy.”

Enki laughed—a sharp, brittle sound. “You still think you get to decide that?”

Enlil narrowed his eyes.

“I made them. I gave them reason; I gave them language.” Enki’s voice was rising now. “And they built something greater than we ever did. And that terrifies you, doesn’t it, brother?”

“You gave them that!” Enlil snapped, levelling his gaze at his brother. “It was you that wanted to dabble with what could be, instead of what was required.” Spittle flew from his lip as Ninlil gently tightened her grip. Enlil looked at her hand, then into her eyes, his features softening. He returned his gaze to the display, “They were meant to serve, not to shape. Not to create.”

“They built this city with their own hands,” Enki said. “Not for you. Not for the Igiggi. But for themselves.”

Enlil’s expression darkened. “It is because they are great, that they must be broken.”

The finality of it. The unshaken resolve. For the first time, Enki turned away from the war-table, staring at the walls, at the grand chamber that had stood for thousands of years. His fists clenched. “You would rather destroy them than admit that they are more than you believe.”

“They are precisely what I believed,” Enlil said. “And if you had not meddled—if you had not given them choice, will—this would never have come to pass.”

A silence stretched between them. One weighted with a thousand years of unspoken truths.

And then—

“The order stands.”

The words fell like a stone into the deep. The first detonations began. In the hologram, the city rippled with fire. Enki watched as the Tower shuddered, as the sky lit with impossible brilliance, as centuries of progress were reduced to nothing in the span of moments. His breath came shallow, his heart pounding against his ribs. It was happening. And he was powerless to stop it.

“They will remember this,” he whispered, voice hoarse.

“They will remember what they were meant to be,” Enlil corrected. “And they will never again reach beyond their station.”

The flickering projection of Babel halted mid-destruction; the war-table’s light frozen against the polished stone of Enlil’s chamber. Enlil’s hand hovered inches above the table, his decree unfinished in the silence. Ninlil’s fingers, once pressing gently into his arm, were locked in place, her expression unreadable beneath the static glow.

But it was Inanna who stood out. Not for the way she was poised, not for her posture—but for her expression. She was smiling. Her lips curled with something that did not belong. It was not approval. Not joy. Not even cruelty.

Cain exhaled slowly, watching the frozen moment with quiet deliberation. He turned to Neba, his face unreadable, the golden light from the war-table casting deep shadows across his features. “This is where it happened,” he said at last.

Neba swallowed, stepping forward, her boots soundless against the polished floor. She turned, slowly, taking in the suspended scene, the faces locked in stillness, the words that had never quite been spoken. She could feel it.

“What is this?” she asked, her voice careful, deliberate.

Cain tilted his head, his gaze shifting from Inanna’s frozen expression to the war-table’s projection. The last instant of Babel’s existence flickered before them, caught between history and erasure.

“A fracture,” he murmured. “One of countless moments in history, a moment where things could have split. Where another path was possible.”

Neba frowned, her fingers flexing at her sides. “Then why didn’t it?”

Cain didn’t answer immediately. He stepped forward instead, his hand passing just inches from Enlil’s rigid form. He studied the face of his once-brother, the unyielding certainty etched into his features. He let out a quiet breath.

“Free will. The decision made, ripples into the future. But this one, this one is different.”

Neba’s brows knit together. “How?”

“This past, however horrific,” Cain gestured to the destruction of Babel, frozen in time by the hologram. “Actually happened. I was there… I witnessed… I participated.”

Neba approached Enki, peering into his eyes. “He was the only one that spoke out. Even Inanna, she just smiled…”

Cain moved to the table, raising his hand, stretching out his fingers to touch the ship on approach. Searching for the ghost of what he had become. “This isn’t a revelation Neba, it is framing. I wanted you to see this, so you can understand what I will show you next.”

“Next?”

“Yes, next.” He dropped his gaze to the floor. “In every moment, we choose, and our lives are the sum of those choices. But sometimes in history, we have the opportunity to see something bigger moving in the shadows. This is one such moment.”

He raised his eyes, searching for hers. “Do you want to see what could have been, if the choices in this room had been different?”

Neba nodded.

Reality collapsed inward, folding, unravelling, stretching into something new.

A long, polished wooden table stretched across the room. Above it, a holographic projection flickered to life—Babel, frozen in its final moment, suspended in devastation.

Enlil, Ninlil, Enki, and Inanna gathered around it, their gazes fixed on the display.

The scene played out exactly as it had previously. Ekis’s objection, Enlil’s cold dismissal. Neba thought they were watching it again in error. Word by word. Moment by moment. Until Enki moved to the side of the table that was more open, away from the bench he had sat on at the beginning.

His fists clenched. “You would rather destroy them than admit that they are more than you believe.”

“They are precisely what I believed,” Enlil said. “And if you had not meddled—if you had not given them choice, will—this would never have come to pass.”

A silence stretched between them. One weighted with a thousand years of unspoken truths.

And then—

Enki moved.

Faster than thought, his arm shot forward, a dagger appeared in Enlil’s chest, so quickly, that its flight from Enki’s hand seemed impossible. Ninlil gasped, her grip on his arm tightening too late, as he slid to the floor. Her face contorted with rage, and she dove for Enki, fingers clawing at the air.

Inanna pounced, tackling Ninlil in mid dive. Her golden hair spilled in wild, unkempt waves over her shoulders, half-matted from the scuffle. The light of the war-table flickered erratically against her skin, casting her sharp features in alternating hues of radiant gold and deepest crimson.

She grinned down at Ninlil, pinning the other woman with one knee pressed hard into her ribs, fingers wrapped tight around her wrist like a lover’s embrace turned violent. There was heat in her stare, a bright, dangerous gleam, as if she were drinking in the impossibility of what had just happened, savouring it like honey on her tongue.

Ninlil writhed beneath her, a guttural, animalistic snarl tearing from her throat. Her delicate, polished veneer was gone—this was something primal, something buried beneath centuries of refinement. Her nails clawed at the floor, at Inanna’s grip, at anything that would reverse what had just happened.

But there was no reversal.

“I hope you have a plan!” Inanna exhaled, her voice breathless with exhilaration, feral with triumph.

Enki was already moving, his hands dancing over the war-table’s controls, fingers flying, a composer orchestrating the impossible. The war-fighters, one by one, peeled away from their descent, the silver fangs of Anunna authority withdrawing from Babel’s sky. The people below, unaware of how close they had come to annihilation, would live to see another sunrise.

Enlil—dead. The war-fighters—recalled. For the first time in millennia, everything had changed.

“We shift to the Abzu,” Enki’s voice was steady, but his hands trembled slightly as her finished his work. He turned toward Inanna, eyes sharp, calculating, alive. “We mine what we need. The moment Nibiru returns, we leave.”

A single beat of silence stretched between them. Then Inanna laughed again—bright, wicked, thrilled.

“Well then,” she purred, flashing Enki a dazzling, reckless smile. “Let’s make sure we don’t waste our second chance.”

Reality folded, focused on the holograph, it zoomed in, the perspective tilting downward as the city thrived beneath them. It was no longer a scene of destruction, no longer the frozen last breath of a civilisation undone.

Instead, it pulsed with life. Towers rose, golden spires catching the sunlight. Roads stretched outward, connecting settlements that flourished. The Adamu did not cower in subjugation—they moved freely, their voices ringing through the streets, their hands shaping the world they had built. Ships did not descend to annihilate them.

Babel had not fallen. It had ascended.

And yet—

The air around Cain and Neba shuddered. The light at the edges of the projection frayed. Something was wrong. Cain exhaled sharply, watching as the perfect image of a world spared began to distort, the very edges of existence trembling as if rejecting the vision entirely.

Neba took a step back. “What’s happening?” she asked.

Cain did not answer. Not yet. Because this—this was the moment he had been waiting for. He turned to Neba, his voice quiet, steady.

“Watch closely,” he said. “You’re about to see the impossible.”

And with that, the world broke.

At first, it was subtle—a tremor beneath the surface of reality, a distortion along the edges of perception. The scene of Babel’s ascension flickers, the light bending at unnatural angles, the world beginning to unthread like a fabric whose weave is coming undone.

Then the illusion shatters.

The city, the golden spires, the thriving civilisation—all of it collapses inward, sucked through an unseen aperture. The roads, the people, the very concept of existence stretches and distorts, unravelling into the smallest blocks of light and numbers. Spiralling, carried as if by an invisible breeze, then dissolving into nothing.

Cain and Neba are no longer standing in the heart of Babel. They are no longer standing anywhere. The space around them flattens, the depth collapsing, perspective reduced to a singularity. The textures of the world dissolve—stone becomes light, bodies become code, and history becomes a mere sequence of indexed references dissolving into a neutral void.

And then they are inside it.

Vast. Infinite. An ocean of reserved potential, existing outside comprehension, outside of understanding. It is both empty and pregnant simultaneously. The beauty, the buildings, the universe itself had been compressed into a latticework, dark clusters almost wormlike pulsed at the edges of the space, as if waiting to be called on.

A space in limbo. Not a graveyard, not anything Neba could describe. And the void comes. True nothingness. The nothingness that was before all things. Cain closes his eyes.

It is coming.

And then—

The Bang.

A rupture in the nothing. A crack in the silence. A single point detonates with absolute force, a singularity screaming into existence, flooding the void with raw, untamed energy.

The birth of a new reality. A universe crawling its way into being. The new reality begins.

Cain does not move. He does not breathe.

He turns to Neba, his voice quiet, but heavy with the weight of eternity.

“Now you understand.”

The vast porch stretched into the early morning stillness, the horizon painted in soft hues of violet and ember as the first light of dawn licked the edges of the savannah. The world here was silent, except for the occasional rustle of wind through the grasses, the distant call of an unseen bird. It was the kind of silence that felt like the pause before an answer—or a question yet unspoken.

Elena inhaled deeply, the scent of the earth rich in the cool air, her fingers resting lightly on her lap. Across from her, Johannes sat with the practiced ease of a man accustomed to stillness. His posture relaxed but precise, his breathing measured. A flicker of gold in his eyes caught the dawn’s light, subtle but present.

She had seen it. Felt it.

Babel.

Not as history had told it. Not as a myth, or a lesson, or an allegory. But as a moment—frozen, examined, rewound. The impossible stretched out before her like a path she had not known existed, and then, just as quickly, it had been taken away.

She turned her head slightly, studying Johannes. His expression remained unreadable, but something in his stillness told her he was waiting.

Finally, she spoke.

“You showed me something,” she said, her voice low, deliberate. “And now you expect me to understand what it meant.”

Johannes exhaled through his nose, a small smile ghosting across his lips. “No, Elena. I showed you what happened, and what could have happened. Understanding… I only expect you to ask the right questions.”

She frowned slightly, fingers pressing together as she searched for the words that would lead her to solid ground. “It wasn’t just propaganda,” she said slowly. “Not just a lie told by the victors. History is always rewritten, I know that much.” She met his gaze. “But this—this was different.”

Johannes nodded once, a single, precise movement. “It was.”

Elena’s pulse thrummed against her ribs. “You knew,” she accused softly. “You suspected that something was wrong. That what we know—the world as it is—wasn’t how it was meant to be.”

Johannes looked away then, his gaze sweeping over the endless stretch of land beyond the porch. For a long moment, he said nothing. Then, “After Babel, Enlil’s focus shifted.”

Elena waited.

“He stopped being concerned about what happened,” Johannes continued, his voice calm, measured. “He became concerned about what was remembered.”

Elena sucked in a breath. The implications of those words settled into her bones like ice. “Erasing history,” she murmured. “Replacing it with something controlled.”

Johannes turned back to her; his gaze sharp, unreadable. “The past is only as real as those who remember it. And when the memory is gone—”

“It never happened,” she finished for him. The realisation made her stomach turn. “That’s what the Council does.”

He didn’t confirm it. He didn’t have to.

Elena ran a hand through her hair, exhaling sharply. “But the last part—the world breaking. That wasn’t rewritten history. That was something else.”

For the first time, Johannes’s expression shifted, the faintest flicker of something resembling hesitation crossing his features. Not uncertainty, not doubt—something deeper.

“I don’t know,” he admitted.

Elena stared at him, startled by the honesty. Johannes never said I don’t know. He theorised, speculated, guided—but he never admitted uncertainty.

“But you have ideas,” she pressed.

He exhaled, leaning back in his chair, fingers laced together in his lap. “I have…assumptions.”

“Which are?”

A moment of silence. Then—

“This isn’t the first time it’s happened.”

Elena’s breath caught in her throat. She had expected a partial answer, a deflection, another question in place of a response. But this—

Johannes’s eyes flickered. “Not just here. Not just with humans. But every time—every time—it happens when the Anunna fade into obscurity.”

The words settled between them. Elena swallowed hard. “So, what are we looking at?”

Johannes’s gaze was distant, lost in a place she could not follow. And when he spoke, it was barely above a whisper.

“A pattern.” He squinted at the sun, “And next year, in 2012, the wheel spins again.”

The wind stirred. The sun crept higher. But for Elena, the world had never felt so still.
